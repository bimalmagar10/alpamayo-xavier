01

The short answer

You asked whether to quantize, distil, or do something else. The honest answer is that those are answers to two different questions, and you need both — but not in the order most people try them.

Quantize to get the model to run. Distil to get it to run fast. The single biggest lever is neither: it is deleting the autoregressive reasoning trace from the runtime path.

On Xavier, generating one token of chain-of-causation reasoning costs about 185 ms in FP16, because every token drags all 15.2 GB of backbone weights across a 136.5 GB/s bus. A 300-token trace is therefore ~56 seconds — 90% of total inference time — before the trajectory decoder has done anything at all. Weight quantization halves or quarters that term. Removing the trace removes it entirely.

Concretely, what I would do

  1. Do not try to run the reference repository. It needs Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.8, bfloat16 and FlashAttention-2. Xavier can supply none of the four, and no upgrade path exists — §3.
  2. Measure the hardware first, then the shapes, then the weights. A shape-faithful surrogate with random weights gives you the real per-stage Xavier latency in a day, with no 22 GB download and no port — because Alpamayo-1 is fully dense, so latency depends only on shapes and dtype. That is Phase 2, and it is the result you can publish even if everything after it stalls.
  3. Then port the real weights in FP16 and validate numerics against an H100 golden run. This is the fidelity result.
  4. Quantize to INT8 via TensorRT 8.5.2, per subgraph. Not FP8, not NVFP4 — Volta has neither, which means NVIDIA's own published Alpamayo quantization recipe does not apply to your board.
  5. Distil a 0.4–1.5 B student on the H100 with a compressed visual token budget and no runtime reasoning. This is the only configuration that reaches interactive rates on Xavier, and it is the architecture NVIDIA themselves describe for production AV compute.

Steps 1–3 are about two weeks. Step 4 is another two. Step 5 is the research contribution and is measured in months.

02

What the model actually is

Published descriptions round Alpamayo-1 to "8.2 B backbone plus 2.3 B action expert." That is too coarse to plan against. The numbers below come from reading the safetensors headers and config.json of nvidia/Alpamayo-R1-10B directly, so every shape here is the shipped one.

Two things in that table change the engineering problem. First, vlm_backend is qwenvl3 — 36 layers at width 4096 with per-head QK-norm, i.e. Qwen3-VL-8B topology, not the Qwen2.5-VL-7B that the Cosmos-Reason lineage implies. Second, the action expert has 36 layers with exactly the backbone's KV geometry (8 heads × 128). That is not a coincidence: it is a π₀-style action expert that attends over the concatenated [backbone KV ; expert KV] at every layer, which means the backbone's KV cache must stay resident while the flow-matching loop runs.

The workload per inference

4 cameras (front-wide, front-tele, cross-left, cross-right) × 4 frames at 10 Hz, downsampled to 320×576. With a 16×16 spatial patch and temporal patch 2, that is 720 patches per frame-slot × 2 slots × 4 cameras = 5,760 ViT tokens, which the 2×2 merger reduces to 1,440 visual tokens entering the LLM. Add 48 history-trajectory tokens and a short text command and the prefill is about 1,550 tokens.

The model then autoregressively emits a chain-of-causation trace followed by 128 discrete trajectory tokens (64 waypoints × quantized acceleration and curvature), and the expert integrates those into 64 continuous waypoints over a 6.4-second horizon.

The backbone KV cache is small — 36 × 8 × 128 × 2 × 2 bytes = 144 KiB per token, so 229 MB at 1,550 tokens. Memory pressure on Xavier comes from weights, not cache.

03

Five blockers, none of them fixable

Your instinct to check dependency compatibility first was right, and the answer is worse than "some pinning work." The reference implementation cannot be made to run on JetPack 5 at all. Each of these is independently fatal.

B1

Xavier is frozen at CUDA 11.4, permanently

JetPack 6 supports Orin only; AGX Xavier tops out at JetPack 5.1.x / L4T r35.x, which pins CUDA 11.4, cuDNN 8.6 and TensorRT 8.5.2. NVIDIA's newest PyTorch wheel for that index is torch-2.1.0a0+41361538.nv23.06-cp38 — I checked the redistributable index directly, and 2.1.0 is the ceiling. The repo wants torch==2.8.0, which requires CUDA ≥ 12. There is no bridge.

B2

The weights are bfloat16; Volta has no bfloat16

config.json declares "dtype": "bfloat16" and 1,162 of 1,166 tensors are BF16. Hardware bf16 arithmetic starts at sm_80. On sm_72 you can hold bf16 tensors but cuBLAS will not issue a bf16 GEMM. Every weight must be cast to FP16, which narrows exponent range from bf16's ~1e38 to 65,504 — so the conversion needs a per-tensor overflow audit, not a blind .half().

B3

FlashAttention-2 does not build for sm_72

pyproject.toml pins flash-attn>=2.8.3 and config.json sets attn_implementation: "flash_attention_2". FA2 requires Ampere or newer. Replace with scaled_dot_product_attention on the memory-efficient backend — which also means you must expand GQA KV heads by hand, since torch 2.1 has no enable_gqa.

B4

Python 3.12 vs. the 3.8 the wheel is built for

The repo pins requires-python = "==3.12.*"; transformers==4.57.1 needs ≥ 3.9. The only CUDA-enabled Jetson PyTorch for JetPack 5 is cp38. Building a newer Python does not help, because there is no matching torch wheel for it and torch ≥ 2.5 will not compile against CUDA 11.4 anyway.

B5

NVIDIA's own Alpamayo deployment path excludes your board

TensorRT-Edge-LLM lists Alpamayo among supported models, but targets Jetson Thor (SM 101), Orin (SM 87), DRIVE GB10 and DGX Spark. Xavier is not on the list. The published quantization recipe (alpamayo1_5_quant, Model Optimizer) produces FP8 and NVFP4 — Hopper and Blackwell formats. Volta supports FP16 and INT8 and nothing newer. You will be writing the Xavier path yourself; that is precisely what makes the measurement worth publishing.

The consequence, stated plainly: there is no configuration in which you pip install your way to Alpamayo on this board. Everything below assumes you re-implement the forward pass against torch 2.1 / cp38 and load the released weights into it. That is roughly 1,500 lines and it is very tractable, because the topology is a well-documented Qwen3-VL decoder plus a π₀-style expert — but it is the actual work item, and pretending otherwise would waste your first month.

04

The Xavier roofline

Before predicting anything, it is worth being precise about which of the two ceilings binds at each stage — because they call for opposite optimizations.

Xavier's Volta GPU offers 11.3 TFLOP/s FP16 (64 tensor cores at 1.377 GHz) and 22.6 TOP/s INT8, against 136.5 GB/s of shared LPDDR4x. The ratio — roughly 83 FLOP per byte — means anything processing many tokens at once is compute-bound, and anything processing one token at a time is bandwidth-bound. Alpamayo does both, in sequence.

Modelled at 45% MFU for large FP16 GEMMs and 60% MBU for streaming reads — conservative but realistic figures for a Volta iGPU under a 30 W envelope. Phase 1 of the plan replaces both constants with measured ones; treat these as the prior, not the result.

Read the decode row twice. At 5.4 tokens per second in FP16, the reasoning trace is not a feature that costs a little extra — it is the entire latency budget, several times over. And it is the one term that quantization attacks most effectively and architecture attacks completely.

The expert is bandwidth-bound for a non-obvious reason: 64 waypoint tokens is far too short a sequence to saturate the tensor cores, so all 10 Euler steps are dominated by re-reading 4.56 GB of expert weights. This is also why enable_diffusion_expert_cuda_graph() exists in the reference code — 36 layers × 10 steps is 360 launches of small kernels, and Xavier's Carmel cores are slow at launch. Use the CUDA graph path; on Xavier it matters more than it does on an H100.

05

Where the milliseconds go

Nine configurations, from the model exactly as NVIDIA ships it down to a distilled student, with the same roofline applied to all of them. These are modelled numbers — the whole point of the plan in §7 is to replace them with measured ones — but the shape of the picture is what matters and that shape is robust to the constants.

Three transitions in that chart carry the entire argument:

  • A → B (FP16 → INT8) buys 1.8×. Real, worth having, and nowhere near enough on its own. Quantization is necessary and insufficient.
  • C → E (stop generating tokens) buys 9.7×. No weight has changed. You simply stopped paying 93 ms for each of 128 trajectory tokens and let the flow-matching expert read the prefill state directly. This is an architecture and training decision, not a runtime flag.
  • E → H (distil the backbone) buys another 14×. And only here does the page cross into anything a robot could use.

Two calibration points against real hardware, for sanity. An 8 B model at Q8 on an AGX Xavier has been reported at roughly 8.4 tokens/s decode on the NVIDIA developer forums, against the 10.8 tokens/s this roofline predicts for INT8 — close enough that the bandwidth model is not fantasy. And on the memory side, an independent 2026 systems paper measures Alpamayo-R1-10B's BF16 footprint at 21.52 GB, against the 22.16 GB in the shard index. The model is consistent with what other people have actually seen.

Here is the full lever set, with what each one actually multiplies. Ranking them by leverage rather than by familiarity is the main thing that separates a six-week project from a six-month one.

So: both, but the reasoning matters

Quantization is what makes the real 10 B model runnable and honest to measure. INT8 halves the weight footprint to 11.1 GB, which takes the model from "22 GB on a 32 GB board with a desktop running, hoping" to comfortable — and gives you a genuine end-to-end Alpamayo-1 latency figure on Xavier silicon. That number does not exist anywhere in the literature right now. It is worth having.

Distillation is what makes it fast. But notice from the chart that most of the distillation win is not from having fewer parameters — it is from a student that has been trained to hand the action expert a plan without narrating it first. NVIDIA describes exactly this split publicly: Alpamayo 1 as a cloud-side teacher, and 0.5–2 B students on production automotive compute. Your Xavier is slower than the DRIVE Orin they have in mind, but the shape of the answer is identical.

What I would not spend time on: CPU offload. The RTX 5070 Ti layer-swapping result that gets cited for Alpamayo works because a discrete GPU has a second, separate pool of host DRAM to stream from. Xavier's CPU and GPU share one 136.5 GB/s LPDDR4x pool, so "offloading" to the CPU moves nothing and buys nothing. NVMe-backed paging would extend capacity at roughly 20× worse bandwidth. Configure swap as a crash net, not as a strategy.

The plan, phase by phase

Ordered so that each phase yields a citable number that survives the next phase failing. Phase 2 in particular gives you the headline result in about three days, without the 22 GB download, without a port, and without needing anything in §3 to be solved.

Phase2

Shape-faithful stage benchmark — the headline result

2–3 daysXavierno weights, no port, no download

This is the phase I would push hardest. alpamayo_stage_bench.py rebuilds Alpamayo-1's exact tensor shapes — 27 ViT blocks at d=1152, 36 decoder layers at d=4096 with 32/8 GQA and QK-norm, 36 expert layers at d=2048 cross-attending into the backbone KV — in FP16 with random weights, and times each stage separately with CUDA events.

Why random weights are legitimate: Alpamayo-1 is fully dense. No mixture-of-experts, no early exit, no data-dependent routing. The wall-clock latency of a dense transformer is a function of shapes, dtype and kernel selection alone. These are not estimates of Xavier latency; they are Xavier latency, obtained without needing Python 3.12, bfloat16 or FlashAttention to exist.

Run the 4-layer and 36-layer versions and compare. If the extrapolation from 4 layers lands within a few percent of the real 36, you have earned the right to sweep the whole design space cheaply — which is what makes the student-model search in Phase 5 affordable.

Audit the bfloat16 → float16 cast before trusting it

Do not blind-.half() 22 GB. BF16 carries FP32's exponent range; FP16 saturates at 65,504 and flushes below 6e-5. Find out which tensors care:

import safetensors.torch as st, glob, torch
FP16_MAX = 65504.0
for shard in sorted(glob.glob("ckpt/alpamayo1/*.safetensors")):
    for name, w in st.load_file(shard).items():
        f = w.float()
        mx, tiny = f.abs().max().item(), (f.abs() < 6e-5).float().mean().item()
        if mx > FP16_MAX * 0.5 or tiny > 0.02:
            print(f"{name:64s} max {mx:12.4f}  subnormal-frac {tiny:.4f}")

Expect the LayerNorm/RMSNorm gains and the vision patch_embed to be the interesting ones. Anything that overflows gets held in FP32 — they are tiny vectors and cost nothing. Anything with heavy underflow gets a per-tensor scale folded into the adjacent norm.

On the Xavier — the port

Take the decoder layer already written in alpamayo_stage_bench.py and give it real weights. The substitutions forced by §3, in full:

  • flash_attention_2 → F.scaled_dot_product_attention under torch.backends.cuda.sdp_kernel(enable_flash=False, enable_mem_efficient=True)
  • GQA → manual repeat_interleave of KV heads by 4; torch 2.1 has no enable_gqa
  • torch.nn.RMSNorm → hand-written, accumulating in FP32; it does not exist before torch 2.4
  • bf16 → fp16 everywhere, with the FP32 exceptions from the audit
  • transformers Qwen3-VL processor → your own preprocessing; do not try to install 4.57 on cp38

Validate layer by layer against the golden dump — cosine similarity per hidden state, then final waypoint L2 in metres. A port that produces plausible-looking trajectories but has a silently wrong RoPE base or a transposed QK-norm is the failure mode here, and only layerwise comparison catches it.

Phase5

Distil a student that fits the board

4–8 weeksH100the only path to interactive rates

Target the design point the chart already identified: a 0.4–1.5 B backbone, ~300 visual tokens, no autoregressive reasoning at runtime, 8 flow steps. That lands between 130 and 480 ms on Xavier — 2 to 8 Hz. Not a 99 ms robotaxi, but a real control loop on 2018 silicon, which is a genuinely interesting claim.

Initialize, don't start over

Since the backbone is Qwen3-VL-8B topology, initialize the student from Qwen3-VL-2B or 4B. Same tokenizer family, same vision-encoder lineage, same DeepStack merger structure — so teacher and student share a vocabulary and logit distillation is direct rather than approximate. Extend the student vocab with the same 4,000 trajectory bins and the six special token ids (151669 onward, from config.json) and you can copy the teacher's trajectory-token embeddings straight across.

Four losses, and drop the third at deployment

  1. Trajectory flow-matching loss against teacher waypoints — the objective that actually matters.
  2. Hidden-state alignment at the expert's input: this is what teaches the student to hand over a usable plan without narrating it.
  3. CoC logit KD on the reasoning trace — train with it so the student learns the causal structure, then never generate the trace at inference. This is the trick that buys the 9.7× in the chart.
  4. Token-budget curriculum: anneal the visual token count down from 1,440 toward ~300 during training rather than truncating a trained model.

Use NVlabs/alpamayo-recipes as the training scaffold — alpamayo1_sft is a HuggingFace Trainer + DeepSpeed setup you can retarget to a student without rewriting the data pipeline. Ignore alpamayo1_5_quant; it emits FP8 and NVFP4, neither of which sm_72 can execute.

Search the student's shape with Phase 2's harness, not with training runs. Every candidate configuration costs a two-minute benchmark on Xavier instead of a GPU-week, so decide depth, width and token budget from measured latency first, then train the one design that fits the budget.

09

Risks and open questions

Things I could not settle from the public record, and where I would expect the plan to bend.

The CoC trace length is unmeasured — and it sets the headline number

The paper describes traces as "concise and causally grounded" but publishes no token-length statistics, and the model card gives none. I assumed 300 generated tokens for configuration A. At 185 ms/token in FP16, every 100 tokens of error moves the total by 18.5 seconds. Measure this on the H100 in Phase 3 — dump the actual trace lengths over a few hundred clips and re-run the roofline. It is a one-line change and it is the largest single uncertainty on the page.

Whether FP16 fits at all without swapping

22.16 GB of weights on a board that reports about 30.9 GiB GPU-accessible, minus a CUDA context (~600 MB–1 GB), minus activations, minus the OS. Headless it should fit; with the desktop running it will not. If Phase 3 thrashes, go INT8 first and treat FP16 as the accuracy reference computed on the H100 rather than a Xavier configuration.

TensorRT 8.5.2 may not fuse a Qwen3-style block cleanly

Per-head QK-norm inside the attention block is newer than TRT 8.5, and 4:1 GQA with an explicit KV-cache input is not a pattern that vintage optimizes well. Expect unfused elementwise chains in --dumpProfile. Mitigation: hand-write a plugin for the fused RMSNorm+RoPE+QK-norm prologue, or accept FP16 for the attention prologue and INT8 only for the four big GEMMs, which is where the bandwidth actually goes anyway.

The 45% MFU assumption is the weakest constant in §4 and §5

Volta iGPU efficiency on tall-skinny GEMMs under a 30 W cap is not well documented, and if the real figure is 30% rather than 45% every compute-bound row grows by half. This is exactly why Phase 1 exists and why it comes before anything expensive. Do not quote §5 numbers externally until Phase 1 has replaced the constants.

Xavier is end-of-life

NVIDIA has announced EOL for the AGX Xavier and Xavier NX families, and JetPack 5 is in sustaining mode. This does not affect the research — measuring a modern VLA on constrained legacy silicon is arguably more interesting because of it — but it does mean no toolchain relief is coming. Everything in §3 is permanent. Plan accordingly, and if a second board is ever an option, an AGX Orin would move you to CUDA 12, TensorRT 10, FP8, and the supported TensorRT-Edge-LLM Alpamayo path in one step.
