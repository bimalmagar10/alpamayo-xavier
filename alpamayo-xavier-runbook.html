01

Yes — but not the way you're picturing it

You asked whether to quantize on the H100 and download the quantized model to the Xavier. Almost: the work splits, and the split is forced by two facts that pull in opposite directions.

Calibration can only happen on the H100. Computing INT8 scales means pushing real driving clips through the full bfloat16 model to observe activation ranges — which needs Python 3.12, torch 2.8, FlashAttention and bfloat16. The Xavier has none of those and never will.

Engine compilation can only happen on the Xavier. A TensorRT .plan is locked to the compute capability, the TensorRT version, and the specific GPU it was tuned on. A plan built on an H100 (sm_90, TRT 10.x) will refuse to deserialize on a Xavier (sm_72, TRT 8.5.2). There is no cross-compilation, no forward compatibility, no workaround.

So what you download is not a quantized checkpoint. It is a set of ONNX graphs with INT8 scales already baked in as Q/DQ nodes, plus the fixtures the Xavier cannot compute for itself. The Xavier turns those into engines locally, once, and keeps them.

04

On the H100

Four stages. The third one contains a verification gate that will refuse to export if the graph math is wrong, and you should let it.

StepA1

Environment and the golden runH100

2–3 hours~25 GB download
source /mnt/DISCL/work/bthapama/alpamayo-xavier/env.sh
alpamayo_check_paths

# login node, first time only: reference venv + Qwen configs
bash "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/setup_h100.sh"

# the reference stack, exactly as NVIDIA pins it
source "$ALPAMAYO_ROOT/alpamayo/ar1_venv/bin/activate"

# golden tensors, fixtures, and the trace-length distribution
python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a1_golden.py" --clips 64 --max-new-tokens 256

No download step: a1 loads straight from $ALPAMAYO_MODEL, and setup_h100.sh verifies the five shards against model.safetensors.index.json before anything else runs — a truncated shard otherwise surfaces as an incomprehensible error two stages later.

Read $ALPAMAYO_ROOT/golden/trace_stats.json before anything else. It settles the largest open number in the whole project: how many reasoning tokens a typical clip actually produces. At 92.6 ms/token on a quantized Xavier, a p50 of 180 means 16.7 seconds of decode; a p50 of 40 means 3.7. Every downstream decision — how much quantization is worth, whether a student is necessary — moves with that one figure.

Deliverablegolden/: inputs, activations at layers 0/8/16/24/35, the predicted waypoints, and measured trace lengths. Plus fixtures/ for the Xavier.
StepA2

Audit the bfloat16 → float16 castH100

20 minutesrun the audit before the cast

Volta has no bfloat16 arithmetic, so every weight must become float16. bfloat16 carries float32's exponent range; float16 saturates at 65,504 and goes subnormal below 6.1e-5. A blind .half() on 22 GB is not safe, it is just usually lucky.

# src defaults to $ALPAMAYO_MODEL, dst to $ALPAMAYO_ROOT/ckpt/alpamayo-fp16
python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a2_cast_fp16.py" --audit-only

# read the promotion list, then commit
python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a2_cast_fp16.py"

Tensors that would overflow or lose more than 2% of their values to subnormals are kept in float32 and recorded in cast_manifest.json. Expect the norm gains and the vision patch_embed to show up; they are small vectors and the float32 promotion costs a few MiB. If a large weight matrix appears in that list, stop and investigate — that is a sign the checkpoint is not what you think it is.

DeliverableA float16 checkpoint plus a manifest saying exactly which tensors were promoted and why.
StepA3

Verify the graph math, then exportH100

1–2 hoursthis is the gate that matters most

graphs.decoder_layer re-expresses a Qwen3 block in operations TensorRT 8.5 can consume — eager attention instead of SDPA, explicit GQA expansion, host-side RoPE. Before exporting anything, a3 runs that re-expression against the real Hugging Face layer and compares:

python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a3_export_onnx.py"

  [ok ] decoder_layer                cos 0.999987  mae 3.1e-05  max 8.4e-04

exporting vision
  wrote vision.onnx  (1.15 GB)
exporting prefill
  wrote prefill.onnx  (13.9 GB)
...
Gate 1If decoder_layer does not match, the script exits and does not export. Do not raise the tolerance. Check, in this order: QK-norm applied per head before RoPE (Qwen3 changed this from Qwen2); the rotate_half convention; repeat_interleave rather than repeat for the 4:1 GQA expansion; and rms_eps = 1e-6. Each of those errors produces trajectories that look completely plausible and are wrong.

Why four graphs and not one

The four subgraphs have genuinely different shapes and precision needs, and TensorRT 8.5 has no notion of a KV cache, so a single model would have to re-run the prompt on every token.

GraphRunsShapeBound by
visiononce[11520, 1536] → [2880, 4096] + 3 DeepStack mapscompute
prefillonce[1, 3000, 4096] → hidden + KV [36,1,8,3000,128]compute
decodeper token[1,1,4096] + cache → logits [1,1,155697]bandwidth
expert10×[1,64,2] + backbone KV → velocity [1,64,2]bandwidth

Two design choices are worth knowing about because they will look odd in the ONNX. RoPE tables are computed on the host and passed in as plain tensors — Qwen3-VL's interleaved 3D mRoPE exports into a Gather/Slice thicket that TRT 8.5 will not fuse, and a 3,000-token table is 1.5 MB. The decode graph returns only its own 1-token KV slice rather than an updated cache, because TensorRT 8.5 cannot alias an input buffer to an output; returning the whole cache would copy ~490 MB per token, while the driver's memcpy of the slice moves 147 KB.

DeliverableFour FP16 ONNX graphs plus shapes.json. Transfer these and get the FP16 path working end to end before touching A4.
StepA4

Calibrate INT8 and bake in the scalesH100

2–4 hoursonly after FP16 verifies on the Xavier

Explicit quantization — Q/DQ nodes written into the graph — beats TensorRT's implicit calibrator here. It is reproducible, you can inspect where the scales landed, it does not depend on TRT 8.5's calibrator matching a modern one, and it means the Xavier needs no calibration data at all.

python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a1_golden.py" --clips 64 --dump-calib
python "$ALPAMAYO_REPO/h100/a4_quantize_int8.py" --calib-clips 64

Calibration inputs must come from real driving clips. Random tensors give ranges that are wrong in both directions — too wide on activations that are actually peaked, too narrow on the outlier channels that matter — and the resulting engine is fast and useless.

Some layers stay FP16 on purpose. The LM head is one enormous GEMM whose quantization error lands directly on token sampling; the norms and the action projections are tiny and numerically sensitive. Excluding all of them costs almost no bandwidth and removes the most likely sources of a silently degraded trajectory.

Deliverable*.int8.onnx with scales embedded, roughly half the size of the FP16 graphs.
05

The transfer

One rsync, and mind the external-data files — an ONNX over 2 GB stores its weights beside it, and copying only the .onnx gives you a graph with no weights that fails at build time with a confusing error.

# on the H100 — note the trailing slashes and the .data glob
rsync -avh --progress --partial \
    "$ALPAMAYO_ROOT/onnx/" \
    bimal@<xavier-ip>:/mnt/ssdhome/models/alpamayo/onnx/

rsync -avh --progress --partial \
    "$ALPAMAYO_ROOT/fixtures/" "$ALPAMAYO_ROOT/golden/" \
    bimal@<xavier-ip>:/mnt/ssdhome/models/alpamayo/

# on the Xavier — confirm nothing landed on the 28 GB eMMC
df -h / /mnt/ssdhome
du -sh /mnt/ssdhome/models/alpamayo/*
ls /mnt/ssdhome/models/alpamayo/onnx/*.data

Over gigabit this is roughly 3 minutes per GB. Use --partial; the Xavier's link will drop at some point during a 19 GB transfer and you do not want to start over.

06

On the Xavier

Four steps, and the first one contains the single detail that stops most people before they start.

StepB1

EnvironmentXavier

30 minutesreusable across all later work
# on the Jetson, from a clone of the same repository
source ./env.sh          # MODELS_ROOT=/mnt/ssdhome/models, ALPAMAYO_WORK=$MODELS_ROOT/alpamayo
bash xavier/setup_xavier.sh
The detail everyone hitsThe venv must be created with --system-site-packages. TensorRT's Python bindings ship as an apt dist-package at /usr/lib/python3.8/dist-packages/tensorrt, and there is no pip-installable TensorRT 8.5 wheel for aarch64/cp38. A plain python3 -m venv hides the system path and import tensorrt fails with a bare ImportError that suggests, wrongly, that TensorRT is missing.

The script refuses to run if /mnt/ssdhome is not on NVMe. That check exists because a missing mount silently redirects 15 GB of engines onto the 28 GB eMMC, and you find out when the root filesystem fills during an engine build.

Note that torch is installed here only for GPU buffer management and the embedding gather. The model itself runs entirely inside TensorRT — no PyTorch model code on the Jetson at all, which is what lets the whole thing work under Python 3.8.

StepB2

Build the enginesXavier

30–90 minutesonce; then keep the plans
# FP16 first, always. It is the reference INT8 gets judged against.
PRECISION=fp16 bash xavier/build_engines.sh

# later, after FP16 verifies
PRECISION=int8 bash xavier/build_engines.sh

The script locks clocks with nvpmodel -m 0 and jetson_clocks before building, which is not cosmetic: TensorRT picks kernels by timing candidate tactics, and a throttling board makes it choose genuinely worse ones that stay in the plan forever.

It builds with --fp16 even for INT8. That flag is the fallback precision for any layer TensorRT declines to run in INT8; without it those layers fall back to FP32 and get dramatically slower.

Then read the profiles. This is the step people skip:

grep -E "Reformat|Myelin|GPU Compute" logs/profile_decode.int8.log | head -40
Gate 2If the decode profile is dominated by Reformat nodes, INT8 is losing to quantization overhead, not winning. TensorRT 8.5 predates Qwen3's per-head QK-norm and does not always fuse the attention prologue, so it can end up converting between INT8 and FP16 around every block. The fix is to exclude the attention prologue from quantization in A4 and keep INT8 only on the four large GEMMs — which is where the bandwidth is anyway. Always compare against the FP16 engine; if INT8 is not faster, say so and use FP16.
StepB3

Verify before you time anythingXavier

15 minutesnon-negotiable
python xavier/verify.py --work "$ALPAMAYO_WORK" --precision fp16
1. vision tower
  [ok ] visual_embeds          cos 0.999912  rel-mae 0.0031  (tol 2e-03)

2. prefill (fed the GOLDEN vision output, to isolate the backbone)
  [ok ] prefill_hidden         cos 0.999604  rel-mae 0.0087  (tol 5e-03)

Each stage is fed the golden input for the stage before it, so a failure localizes instead of cascading. If vision passes and prefill fails, the fault is in the backbone graph or its scales, not in preprocessing.

Gate 3Every failure mode that matters here — a wrong RoPE base, a transposed QK-norm, GQA expanded with repeat instead of repeat_interleave, INT8 scales calibrated on the wrong tensor — still produces smooth, plausible-looking trajectories. Cosine similarity against the reference is the only thing that catches them. A latency number for a model computing the wrong thing is worse than no number, because you will believe it.

Then run the configuration that matters most:

python xavier/run_alpamayo.py --work "$ALPAMAYO_WORK" \

--no-reasoning skips the chain-of-causation rollout and hands the prefill state straight to the flow-matching expert. That is not a hack: the reference implementation already masks all 4,000 discrete trajectory tokens out of the rollout, so the expert has always been the thing that produces the trajectory. Skipping the trace should take you from ~30 s to ~6.4 s, and it costs planning quality on exactly the long-tail cases the model was built for — which is the trade to quantify, not to assume.

What you should see

Modelled at 45% MFU and 60% MBU. If your measurements land within about 30% of these, the pipeline is behaving; if decode is 3× off, something is wrong before you start optimizing.

Corrected against the reference source: 16 images at 720 patches each give 2,880 visual tokens and a ~3,000-token prefill, and the 128 trajectory tokens are never decoded — ExpertLogitsProcessor masks them for the whole rollout.
ConfigurationVisionPrefillDecodeExpertTotalWeights
FP16, 256 reasoning tokens2.81 s9.24 s47.4 s0.61 s60.1 s20.9 GB
INT8, 256 reasoning tokens1.41 s4.62 s23.7 s0.33 s30.1 s10.4 GB
INT8, trace capped at 961.41 s4.62 s8.89 s0.33 s15.2 s10.4 GB

The shape of that table is the finding. Quantization halves everything, which is real but is not the story. Dropping the reasoning rollout is worth 4.7×, and unlike quantization it is a runtime flag. And once reasoning is gone the model is compute-bound in vision and prefill — at which point the lever stops being bits and becomes visual tokens, which is why the distilled student in the feasibility study pairs a smaller backbone with a compressed tokenizer rather than more aggressive quantization.

Per-token decode should come out near 185 ms in FP16 and 93 ms in INT8. That figure is almost pure memory bandwidth — 15.2 GB and 7.6 GB of weights respectively over a 136.5 GB/s bus — so it is the most predictable number in the table and the best early check that your INT8 engine is genuinely running in INT8.

One honest caveat about the code. Every file is shape-checked and the logic follows the reference implementation closely, but none of it has been run against the real 22 GB checkpoint — I do not have your H100 or your Jetson. That is precisely why the three gates are structured the way they are: Gate 1 catches an error in my graph re-expression before you spend two hours exporting, and Gate 3 catches an error in the engines before you spend a week reporting numbers from them. Treat a gate failure as information about the code, not as a tolerance to loosen.

10

Order of work

Two rules, both learned by other people the expensive way.

  1. FP16 end to end before INT8 anywhere. An INT8 engine that is wrong and an FP16 port that is wrong look identical from the outside, and if you build both at once you cannot tell which one broke. Get FP16 through Gate 3, record its latency, and only then quantize — with FP16 as the accuracy reference.
  2. Run bench/ first, in parallel with all of this. The shape-faithful stage benchmark needs no weights, no ONNX, no engines and no H100. It gives you measured per-stage Xavier latency for Alpamayo's exact shapes in about three days. If the pipeline above stalls for a fortnight on a TensorRT fusion problem — a realistic outcome with an 8.5-era toolchain and a 2026 model — you still have the number that the work was for.

And record the board state with every run: power mode, jetson_clocks, torch/TRT versions, precision, prefill length, generated tokens, flow steps, p50 and p95 per stage, peak allocation, and whether swap moved. Four precisions across several model sizes over months is a lot of numbers, and the ones lacking metadata are not comparable to each other later.
